# Case 12

## Example chapter

The tide came in under the Salt Archive twice a day, and Suvi Arkanen had learned to hear it before she saw it: a low, patient knock in the foundation stones, like someone politely asking to be let in. At half past five on a grey Tuesday she stood on the landing above the Upper Reading Room in her wading boots and counted the knocks, as she had counted them every morning for eleven years, and when they reached seven she turned and went down to open the sluices.

She was thirty-three, small and sunburnt in a city that saw very little sun, with black hair cut short so it wouldn't hold the damp, and a long white scar across the back of her right hand from a vault door that had closed before she did. The archivists of Saltwick wore grey smocks with a salt-white collar. Suvi wore hers over a fisherman's jersey and had long ago stopped apologizing for it.

The Archive was the oldest building on the waterfront and the only one built to drown on purpose. Its lower vaults sat below the high-tide line, sealed in stone and lined with cured vellum that the salt kept from rotting; its upper floors stayed dry; and between them ran the Tidegate, a long iron corridor of sluices that let the sea in and out by the clock. Every record in the city lived somewhere in that slope. The newest, deeds and shipping lists, rode in the dry stacks near the Reading Room. The oldest lay far below, where the water reached them twice a day and left them better preserved than before.

"You're early," said Bertrand Okoye, who was always already there.

He was the Keeper, sixty-one and heavy, with a square-cut white beard and a way of standing in doorways as though the doorway had been his idea. He kept his desk in the Reading Room's north window, under the one lamp in the Archive that was permitted to burn, and he had never once, in Suvi's hearing, raised his voice.

"The tide's at seven-forty," she said. "I wanted the sluices set before the Warden's clerk comes to count."

"The Warden's clerk is already counting." He nodded toward the street door, where a thin young man in the green sash of the Harbor Assembly was copying the morning's gate readings into a ledger. "Don't look at him. He takes it personally."

Suvi looked at him anyway. His name was Pell Dunmore, he was twenty-six, and he had a habit of writing with his tongue between his teeth that made it hard to dislike him as much as the Assembly's sash required. He lifted two fingers to her without raising his head.

"Morning, Pell," she called.

"Archivist," said Pell, to the ledger.

The rule of the Archive was written over the Reading Room door in letters worn nearly smooth: no open flame below this line. Every child in Saltwick knew it. The old stacks held three centuries of paper and vellum, and a single dropped candle had once burned out the whole of the east wing before the Archivists' Chapter had even been founded. The Keeper's lamp was the one exception, and it stood inside a glass chimney that had to be inspected every Friday by someone who was not the Keeper.

Suvi set the sluices, one wheel at a time, feeling each iron gate seat itself in its groove. By the time she climbed back up, the Reading Room had begun to fill: clerks from the shipping houses, a woman from the Chandlers' Guild with a sheaf of lease papers, two old men who came every Tuesday to read the harbor deeds aloud to each other and disagree. The air smelled of salt and lamp oil and wet wool. Somewhere below them, the sea began to arrive.

The courier came at nine. Lio Varga was twenty-four, long-legged and wind-flushed, with a satchel across his chest and a green armband that meant he ran messages for the Assembly. He took the Reading Room stairs two at a time and stopped at Suvi's desk out of breath, grinning, holding out a flat wooden box tied with black cord.

"For the Lower Vaults," he said. "Vault Four. The Warden's own hand."

"Vault Four is closed to anything but Chapter business."

"It says Chapter business on the lid." He turned it over. Across the wood, in a clerk's neat copperplate, someone had written: To be shelved unread. By order of the Warden. "I'm only the legs."

Suvi took the box. It was heavier than it looked, and it rattled once, faintly, like a key in a drawer. She signed Lio's receipt, and he winked at her and was gone, taking the stairs down the way he'd come up, and the old men at the reading table paused their quarrel to watch him go.

"Don't open it," said Okoye, from the north window.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were turning it over with great interest."

"Only to find the weight." She set it down on the edge of the desk. "Vault Four is on the tidal side, Keeper. It floods at nine-forty. If I'm to shelve this, I go now."

He regarded her for a long moment over his half-moon glasses. "Take the Sounding Key," he said finally. "Not the ring. The Sounding Key."

That surprised her. The Sounding Key was an iron key as long as her hand, hung on a cord behind Okoye's desk, and in eleven years she had seen it used exactly twice. It opened the oldest doors in the Archive, the ones that had been locked before the Chapter existed, and Okoye kept it where only he could reach it.

"Why?"

"Because I'd rather you didn't need to ask anyone for another." He took it down himself, wound the cord twice around her wrist, and tucked the end under her cuff. "Shelve the box. Read nothing. Come straight back up."

The descent began where the dry stacks ended, at a stone stair that spiraled down into the Tidegate. Suvi went down it with a sealed brass lamp that gave light without flame, and the box under her arm, past the first sluice chamber, then the second, her boots ringing on wet iron. The Tidegate was a long, cold throat. Seawater moved somewhere beyond the walls with a sound like slow breathing. Every sixth step she passed a painted number, and at forty she reached the door to the Lower Vaults, black with age and bound in bands of green copper.

Vault Four lay at the end of the third corridor. It was a long, low room of cedar shelves, the oldest in the Archive, and its air tasted of brine and of something older, a sweetness like pressed flowers. A single stone ledge ran around the wall at knee height, where the tide would come, and above it the shelves held volumes in vellum bindings gone the color of tea. Over the door, carved into the lintel, was the date the Archive had been founded, and beneath it, smaller: Nothing here is a copy.

She had never been in this vault alone.

She found the shelf marked for the Warden's deposits and slid the box into place, and then, because the cord at her wrist had caught on the corner of the next shelf and tugged her sleeve back, she saw the book.

It sat one shelf down, spine out, thicker than its neighbors, and its title was stamped in gold that the salt had never dulled: The Charter of Saltwick, original text.

Suvi stood very still.

The Charter of Saltwick hung in the Assembly Hall, framed behind glass, and every child in the city learned its first article by heart: that the harbor and all its quays were held in trust by the Harbor Assembly, on behalf of the people of Saltwick, for as long as the Assembly kept faith with them. It was the reason the Assembly set the dock fees. It was the reason Pell Dunmore wore a green sash. It was the reason the Warden had a seat.

She did not mean to open it. She told herself so afterwards. But the book fell open under her hand to the first page of the first article, and the vellum was warm from the stone, and she read:

The harbor and its quays are the common property of the guilds of Saltwick, to be governed by a council of their elected masters.

There was no Assembly in it. There was no trust.

She read it again, and then a third time. Her hands had begun to shake, so she set the lamp on the ledge, and in that moment the sea moved against the wall behind her, one long, heavy knock, and the lamp's light jumped.

"Nine-thirty," she said aloud, to nobody. "I have ten minutes."

She did not take the book. Okoye's instruction had been plain: shelve and go. But she took the page's measure with her eyes, the clause, the date in the margin, the name of the clerk who had set it down, a woman called Ilse Marrick, and she fixed them in her mind the way she fixed everything, by counting.

The door at the end of the corridor opened.

Light came first, steady and white, then the sound of two sets of boots. Suvi closed the book, slid it back into its place, and stepped away from the shelf with the Warden's box in plain view and her face arranged into the expression of someone who had been looking for the right ledge.

"Archivist Arkanen," said Hesper Quill.

The Warden was fifty-five, tall and spare, in the Harbor Assembly's green greatcoat with the Warden's silver knot at the shoulder. She had a long, pleasant face and a voice that never rose or hurried, and she came into Vault Four as though she had been invited. Behind her, wearing the sash and a look of acute embarrassment, came Pell Dunmore with a ledger under his arm.

"Warden," Suvi said.

"I understand you've been entrusted with a deposit." Quill's eyes went to the box on its shelf, and something in her shoulders eased, very slightly. "Thank you. I'd have brought it myself, but the Keeper insists on his rules."

"Chapter business only, below the Reading Room," said Suvi.

"So he says." The Warden smiled. "He is very careful, Bertrand. It's why we trust him."

Pell cleared his throat. "Warden, the tide—"

"I know the tide, Pell. I have rung its bell for twenty years." She said it lightly, and Pell looked at the floor, because everyone in Saltwick believed that the great Tide Bell in the Bellhouse rang by itself before a storm, a miracle of the old harbor, and hardly anyone in the city knew that the Warden rang it by hand from a ledger of weather reports. Suvi had learned it two winters ago, by accident, and had told no one.

"If you don't mind," Quill went on, "I'll take the deposit. The Assembly would like it kept at the Hall for the present."

It was not a request. Suvi stepped aside. The Warden lifted the box, weighed it in her hands, and the faint rattle came again, like a key.

"Thank you, Archivist," she said. "You've been very helpful."

She turned for the door. Pell hesitated half a step, glanced at the shelf where the Charter sat, and then, with the unhappy conviction of a man who has decided to look at nothing, followed her out.

Suvi stood alone in Vault Four with the water beginning to come over the ledge. It crept in around her boots, cold and quiet, and she counted the knocks until she reached seven and then climbed back out through the Tidegate with her heart going like a sluice wheel.

Okoye was waiting at the head of the stair. He did not ask what she had seen. He took the Sounding Key from her wrist, unwinding the cord with great care, and held it a moment in his broad hand.

"The Warden took the box," she said.

"I know. I sent for her myself."

"You—" She stared at him. "Keeper, the Charter in Vault Four—"

"Is the Charter," said Okoye. "Every copy that hangs in a hall is a copy. This one is not." He looked at her over the half-moon glasses, and for the first time in eleven years she saw how tired he was. "The Chapter has kept it for three hundred years, Suvi. It is not ours to hide and it is not ours to show. It is ours to keep, until someone is ready to be trusted with it."

"And the Warden?"

"The Warden knows it exists. She does not know it is shelved one shelf below her own deposit." He closed his hand around the key. "Go and eat something. The tide is up. You have the afternoon."

She went. She walked out through the Reading Room, past the old men reading their deeds, past the woman from the Chandlers' Guild, past the glass-chimneyed lamp burning in the north window, and stood for a long time on the Archive's front step with the sea slapping at the quay stones and the whole grey waterfront of Saltwick running away before her.

Out over the harbor, the Tide Bell began to ring, though there was no storm and the Warden was somewhere in the Hall with a wooden box. Someone else was ringing it. She counted the strokes. Seven.
