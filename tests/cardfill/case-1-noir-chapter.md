# Case 1

**Give the pipeline:** an example chapter only. No notes.

## Example chapter

The Lantern Bar had a low ceiling and a lower opinion of daylight, and at ten in the morning it held exactly one customer, a dockhand asleep on his elbows. Walt Orsolo was polishing a glass that had been clean since Tuesday. When I came in he set it down like a man caught at something.

"Ines," he said. "You came."

"You left four messages with my landlady. She thinks I owe you money."

"It's the other way round." He tried a smile. It didn't hold. "Sit. I'll pour you something."

"It's ten in the morning."

"I'll pour you something anyway."

I took the stool at the end of the bar, where the wood was worn pale by other people's elbows. Walt poured rye into a coffee cup, which I took for tact, and set a second cup beside it with nothing in it. I'd known him fifteen years. He had never once passed up a drink on a bad day.

"It's the Mutual," he said.

I had figured as much. Everybody on Quay Street paid the Harbor Mutual, and everybody had a different word for it. It had started as a burial society for dockworkers' widows, back when the cranes killed a man a month, and somewhere along the way the burial fund had learned to collect from the living. Dues, they called it. A flat sum on the first Friday of every month, handed to a man in a grey coat, and in return nothing happened to you. That was the whole product. Nothing happened.

"Dues went up," I said.

"Doubled, since March." He turned the empty cup a quarter turn. "I can make this Friday. Maybe. And it won't be one of the boys who comes. It'll be Tobias Lund himself."

"Lund doesn't collect."

"No. He counts." Walt looked at the dockhand, who hadn't moved. "Dolly Pruitt's place on Fenn Street, he came in person the month before it closed. Her lease went to a man nobody had heard of. Lund shook her hand on the way out. She told me he thanked her for her patience."

That was how it worked, then. Squeeze a place until the books looked bad, then send somebody polite to offer to take the lease off your hands for the price of what you owed. The Mutual didn't break windows. It bought them.

"What do you want from me, Walt?"

"I want you to find out where the money goes."

"It goes to Lund."

"It goes through Lund." He lowered his voice, though the dockhand's snore hadn't changed. "Maureen kept their books for two years before she died. You remember Maureen."

I remembered Maureen, his wife's cousin, a small, careful woman who'd once corrected my arithmetic at this very bar. "I remember her."

"She told me something near the end. She said the burial fund is empty. Has been since before the war. Everything we pay goes out the same day, in an envelope, to somebody upstairs at the Customs House. Lund just carries it." He wiped his hands on the bar towel, though they were dry. "She never saw a name. Only initials on a receipt. And she made me swear not to tell anyone, and I haven't. Not Dov, not anyone."

I put my cup down.

Dov Haskell was a sergeant in the harbor police and, for twenty-two years, my brother-in-law, until Ames died and I stopped being anybody's sister-in-law but his. Dov would have been the obvious door to knock on. He had also walked the same beat for a decade without once writing a Mutual man a ticket, and I had stopped asking why around the time I stopped being invited to Christmas.

"You're right not to tell Dov," I said.

"That's what I figured."

"And what's upstairs at the Customs House? Forty clerks and a flag."

"I don't know. That's what I'm paying you to learn." He glanced at the door, then reached into his waistcoat and drew out something that made me sit up. He laid it on the bar between the two cups: a silver pocket watch, dented at the edge, the chain wound twice around itself.

"My father's," he said. "Worth more than what I owe you, a good deal more. Hold it till I can pay."

"Walt, I don't take collateral."

"You do today."

I picked the watch up. It was heavier than it looked and warm from his pocket, and on the back someone had scratched a row of tiny marks that might have been tally lines or might have been a name. I didn't ask. A man who hands you his father's watch is handing you something he doesn't want to be asked about.

Behind me the door opened, and the bar changed.

I have seen rooms go quiet before. This was different. The regulars didn't stop talking, because there weren't any, but the air thickened, the way it does when a certain kind of coat walks in. Walt's hand went to the towel. The dockhand, who I'd have sworn was out cold, quietly turned his chair so his back was to the wall.

I didn't look around. I put the watch in my coat and finished the rye.

"Thursday," I said to Walt, low. "Not Friday. You'll hear from me Thursday. Don't tell anyone I was here."

"Ines—"

"Pour that man a drink," I said, "and smile when you do it."

I stood, and on my way out I passed the grey coat in the doorway, a boy of about twenty with a clerk's soft hands and a hard, patient face. He held the door for me. It was the politest thing that had happened to me all week, and I walked the whole length of Quay Street with the feeling that I had been counted.
